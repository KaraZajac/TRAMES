\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Automated licence-plate readers (\alpr) have spread from tollbooths to residential streets. We
measure what that means for an ordinary trip, and how fast it is changing. A continental
routing engine models each camera's field of view and can find a route that crosses none. We
route \num{56131} home--work commutes from all fifty states and the District of Columbia, with
and without avoidance, past \num{186316} installations: \num{142257} mapped by volunteers in
OpenStreetMap, and \num{44059} more in service that only the registry of the dominant vendor,
Flock Safety, records. \SI{78.6}{\percent} of commuters pass at least one reader on the way
to work. Refusal is
still cheap: \SI{80.8}{\percent} can reach work past none, and avoiding adds a median of
\SI{2.29}{\minute}---about two-fifths more than the volunteer map alone implies. OpenStreetMap
keeps every edit, so we also rebuild its camera map as it stood on the first of every month
since January 2024, node for node, and re-ask the question of each on the same commutes and
roads. The map grew 128-fold in thirty-three months, and exposure almost in proportion: the
cameras an average commute passes rose 94-fold, and the share of commuters passing one from
\SI{2.2}{\percent} to \SI{75.3}{\percent}. Refusal grew dearer per camera as well as in
total: on the first map of 2024 \SI{95}{\percent} of exposed commuters could reach zero, for a
median of \SI{0.8}{\minute}; on today's map \SI{79}{\percent}
can, for \SI{3.1}{\minute}, and \SI{75}{\percent} once the vendor's registry is added, for
\SI{3.9}{\minute}. What a commute passes, and what declining costs, are functions of a map that
is still filling in; we say when ours was drawn.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} automated licence-plate recognition; surveillance;
route choice; volunteered geographic information; OpenStreetMap; edit history.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automated licence-plate readers (\alpr) photograph every passing vehicle, resolve the plate,
and retain the read \citep{finklea2024}. Unlike a traffic camera they are not triggered by an offence, and unlike
a patrol officer they do not tire. Fixed, pole-mounted units have spread from limited-access
highways into residential neighbourhoods, arterial roads, and the entrances of private
subdivisions \citep{lum2019,stanley2022}.

The public debate about this expansion is conducted in the vocabulary of policy: retention
windows, warrant requirements, audit logs, data-sharing agreements. What that vocabulary
lacks is a measurement of the thing being regulated. How much of this does a person
actually drive through on the way to work on an ordinary Tuesday? And if they would rather
not, is declining a practical option or a theoretical one?

This paper answers both, and does so by building the instrument the question implies. We
construct a routing engine that treats \alpr fields of view as first-class geometry and
can be asked for a route that avoids them. Running each sampled commute through it
twice---once normally, once avoiding---yields the exposure and the price of refusing it, for
the same trip, on the same network, at the same speeds.

The second quantity is where the paper earns its keep. A privacy protection that costs
four minutes is a feature. One that costs forty is available only to people with forty
minutes to spare. We therefore report the price of refusal as a distribution rather than a
point: for most commuters it is a few minutes, and for a tail it is not.

The third question follows from the data source, and it is the one this paper is about. The
cameras are mapped by volunteers, and the map has grown 128-fold since January 2024.
OpenStreetMap keeps every edit, so the measurement can be repeated on the map as it stood at
any past instant. We repeat it on the first of every month for thirty-three months: the same
commutes on the same roads, re-scored against each month's cameras and re-routed against each
quarter's. How much surveillance a commuter drives through, and what declining it costs, turn
out to be functions of how much of it had been mapped---and both have risen since, exposure
on all but one of the monthly maps and the cost on every routed map from July 2024.

A volunteer map is also a lower bound, and for the present it can be checked against the
vendor's own records. Flock Safety's device registry as it stood in December 2025 has been
published; merged with the map, it adds \num{44059} in-service readers in the fifty states and
the District that no mapper had reached, nearly a third again. The current figures in this
paper rest on that merged map. The trend, which a registry without dates cannot join, rests on
the map alone, and the difference between the two is itself a measure of what the volunteer
map still misses.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A directional exposure model at continental scale.} Cameras are wedges,
    not circles. \SI{98.0}{\percent} of mapped North American \alpr installations carry a
    bearing tag, making this feasible nationally; proximity counting would charge drivers
    for cameras aimed at the opposite carriageway.
  \item \textbf{Avoidance routing with no forked router.} Camera fields of view---the map's
    \num{157084} cones, merged where they overlap into \num{135210} polygons, and with the
    registry's \num{44176} discs \num{159286}---are
    compiled into a stock GraphHopper spatial index at import time, so an ordinary per-request
    custom model can reference them by name.
  \item \textbf{A commute-weighted sample.} \num{56131} real home--work pairs in all fifty
    states and the District of Columbia, drawn from LEHD LODES with probability proportional
    to worker count and weighted to each state's commuters.
  \item \textbf{The camera map at any instant, from the edit history.} Every version of every
    node ever tagged as a reader, extracted once from OpenStreetMap's full-history dump and
    validated node for node against the public history server, gives the map on the first of
    every month since January 2024 and the day each camera entered it. The map's coverage is
    checked against the counts \num{1050} police departments publish, of which it holds a
    median \SI{98}{\percent}.
  \item \textbf{The map, merged with the vendor's own registry.} Flock Safety's device
    records as they stood in December 2025, paired one-to-one with the map within
    \SI{50}{\metre}: \num{88754} registry devices stand on a mapped node, and the \num{44059}
    in-service plate readers in the fifty states and DC that the map lacks enter the current
    picture as \SI{60}{\metre} discs, the registry recording no bearing. They raise the price
    of refusal by two-fifths. The current figures rest on this merged map; the trend, which an
    undated registry cannot join, on the map alone.
  \item \textbf{A thirty-three-month trend on a fixed sample.} The same commutes on the same
    roads against thirty-three monthly maps, re-scored for exposure on each and re-routed for
    avoidance on each quarter's: exposure rose almost in proportion to the map, and refusal
    grew dearer per camera as well as in total.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

This paper sits where three bodies of work meet: research on \alpr as a policing technology, on
volunteered geographic data and its biases, and a small practice of routing around surveillance.
We take them in turn, and then state what is, to our knowledge, new here.

\paragraph{\alpr as a policing technology.} Criminological research on \alpr has mostly asked
whether it works: \citet{lum2011} tested \alpr-equipped patrols in a place-based randomized
design, \citet{lum2025} review the impact studies since, and \citet{shjarback2025} evaluate
Atlantic City's fixed readers on every entrance and exit to the city. Sociological and legal
work asks what the technology does to policing itself---plate readers lowering the threshold
for inclusion in police data \citep{brayne2017}, the automated suspicion they enable
\citep{joh2016,ferguson2017}, and Flock's network as an infrastructure through which policing
crosses jurisdictions \citep{monahan2026}---and the legal status of the location records they
produce is unsettled \citep{carpenter2018,finklea2024}. None of this work measures how much
\alpr surveillance an ordinary trip passes, or how fast that is changing.

\paragraph{Volunteered geographic data.} The camera map is volunteered geographic information
\citep{goodchild2007}, whose quality has been assessed against authoritative data since
OpenStreetMap's early years \citep{haklay2010} and whose completeness is known to vary with place
and development \citep{herfort2023}. For surveillance specifically, the Atlas of Surveillance
records which agencies use which technologies but not where devices stand \citep{atlas}; DeFlock
supplies the locations \citep{deflock}. Coverage bias is the central threat to every result built
on such data, and we know of no earlier measurement of it for \alpr; we compare the map with the
camera counts police departments publish in Flock's transparency portals
(\Cref{sec:coverage}), and its own history shows how far it was from complete on any earlier date.

\paragraph{Exposure along the way.} That exposure to a place-bound feature depends on where
people go and not only on where they live is the uncertain geographic context problem
\citep{kwan2012}; measuring surveillance along the commute rather than at the home address is
an answer to it, and the unit of analysis throughout is the trip.

\paragraph{Routing around surveillance.} A route that avoids cameras is an older idea than
\alpr: in 2001 iSee computed ``paths of least surveillance'' around Manhattan's closed-circuit
cameras \citep{isee2001,schienke2002}, of which a civil-liberties survey had counted some
\num{2400} visible from the street \citep{nyclu2006}. Several services now route drivers around mapped
\alpr, some using each camera's field of view \citep{deflockmaps,flockhopper,banditkin,driversagainstflock},
and in network measurement \citet{edmundson2016} characterize, and try to avoid, Internet paths
through surveillance states. None publishes a measurement of how much surveillance ordinary
trips pass or what avoiding it costs; the routing engine here is the instrument for that
measurement, not its contribution.

\paragraph{What is new here.} To our knowledge this paper is the first to measure, nationally
and on real commute flows, how many \alpr fields of view commuters pass and what avoiding them
costs; the first to reconstruct the crowdsourced \alpr map from OpenStreetMap's edit record at
monthly resolution and follow a fixed sample of trips across it; and the first to check that
map's coverage against the camera counts police departments publish.

\section{The instrument}
\label{sec:system}

The measurement needs a router that treats ``avoid these \num{159286} polygons'' as a cost
rather than a post-hoc filter. GraphHopper 11.0 \citep{graphhopper11} supplies it without a
fork: it resolves a directory of custom areas into a spatial index \emph{at graph import
time}, and---the documentation is ambiguous, so we verified it---an area registered that way
can be referenced by name from a \emph{per-request} custom model:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=]
{ "priority": [ { "if": "in_alpr", "multiply_by": 0.01 } ] }
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
Shipping \num{159286} polygons in every request is not viable; naming them is. The cost is
that a new cone set needs a full re-import, about two hours for North America. Two settings
follow. Contraction Hierarchies \citep{geisberger2008} are disabled, since they bake edge
weights into the prepared graph and a per-request avoidance strength needs them mutable;
landmarks, the ALT algorithm of \citet{goldberg2005}, serve instead. And the avoiding profile
shares the plain profile's landmark preparation, which is valid only because avoidance
multiplies priority strictly downward, so the landmark distances remain the lower bounds ALT
requires. The map's cone geometry also serves TRAMES, a fork of the OsmAnd navigation client
\citep{osmand} that routes around the cameras on the phone itself: its offline maps tag every
road a cone touches, and OsmAnd's own router penalises those roads with the same multipliers. A
public instance of this engine served the app's optional online mode until 29 August 2026; the
study's routing server binds to localhost and is not internet-facing. The app avoids the
volunteer map alone: the registry's readers that this paper's current figures count
(\Cref{sec:data:registry}) are not in it, and \Cref{sec:discussion} measures what that leaves
on a route planned around the map alone.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

All sources are public and key-free.

\subsection{ALPR installations}
\label{sec:data:alpr}

Source: OpenStreetMap \citep{osm}, queried through the Overpass API \citep{olbricht2015} for nodes tagged
\texttt{man\_made=\allowbreak surveillance} with \texttt{surveillance:\allowbreak type=ALPR},
matched case-insensitively (19 readers are tagged \texttt{alpr})---the corpus surfaced by the
DeFlock project, taken from the upstream rather than a mirror. Retrieved in five regional
queries (continental US, Alaska, Hawaii, Canada, Mexico) and deduplicated by OSM node id.
The map in this paper is the snapshot of \textbf{2026-09-22: \num{142991} unique camera
nodes}, \num{142257} of them in the 50 states and the District of Columbia; the rest fall in
the parts of Canada and Mexico the regional queries cover. With the registry's readers
(\Cref{sec:data:registry}) the merged map holds \num{186316} installations in the states and
the District (\Cref{fig:map}). A first snapshot, taken 2026-07-24, held \num{120838} (\num{120272} in the states),
and \Cref{sec:data:history} reconstructs the map on the first of every month since January
2024.

A fetch that fails over between Overpass mirrors has to check the mirror's database date: on
the first attempt one regional query was answered from a mirror four months stale, and roughly
\num{13600} cameras silently reverted to old tags with nothing reported as failed. The fetch
now rejects any answer older than 48 hours, and every tile of the snapshot used here falls
within a 14-minute window.

Three tagging realities shaped the parser. Mappers write the bearing under plain
\texttt{direction}, not the \texttt{camera:direction} the wiki documents
\citep{osmwikisurveillance}, by a factor of 60; reading only the documented key would discard
\SI{98}{\percent} of the bearings. Explicit arcs are real and mostly \ang{45}: \num{4632}
range values on \num{4372} nodes, \num{3806} of them (\SI{82}{\percent}) spanning exactly
\ang{45}, which is what justifies \ang{45} as the default span for the \SI{97}{\percent} of
cameras that give a bare bearing---the default is inherited from the data, not assumed. And
multi-head units exist (\texttt{320;190}, \texttt{0;72;144;216;288}): \num{9150} nodes,
typically watching both carriageways, so the parser emits one wedge per head rather than
silently taking the first, which would make a camera that sees everyone look like one that
sees half.

\subsection{Flock Safety's device registry}
\label{sec:data:registry}

For the present, the volunteer map can be supplemented with the vendor's own records. An
independent researcher has published a map of Flock Safety's device registry as it stood in
December 2025 \citep{michael2026,mazurov2026}, which we retrieved on 25 September 2026. Its
\num{335701} records, \num{334525} of them in the fifty states and the District, give each
device's position, type (a \emph{Falcon} reads plates; other types record video or sound),
status (in service, planned or decommissioned), a label that often names the road and the
traffic watched (\texttt{\#02 Pacific Pointe (Outbound)}), the date its record was created,
and a \texttt{rotationAngle}, which is not a bearing: on the \num{73226} paired devices whose
mapped node carries one surveyed bearing, the two disagree by a median of \ang{90}, as two
independent angles would, and agree within \ang{20} for \SI{7.8}{\percent}. No direction is
derived from it.

Each registry device is paired with at most one mapped node, and each node with as many
devices as it has heads, within \SI{50}{\metre}, nearest first, the two sides' vendor and
capability required to agree: \num{88754} pairs, covering \SI{63.8}{\percent} of the
registry's \num{126389} in-service plate readers, at a median separation of
\SI{5.6}{\metre}. \num{54372} mapped nodes have no registry device within reach---another
vendor's camera, or one the registry does not carry---and \num{45762} in-service plate readers
in the registry have no mapped node. We drop \num{1586} of those whose records place them at a
coordinate shared by many devices (a building's address rather than a pole, \num{1473}), name
them as Flock's own or test units, say they are retired, or duplicate another record, and add
the remaining \textbf{\num{44176}} to the map: \num{44059} in the fifty states and the
District, 117 in Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands, where no sampled commute runs. Planned
and decommissioned readers, and devices that do not read plates, stay out. The registry adds
only Flock's devices, so on the merged map Flock's share of installations rises from
\SI{80}{\percent} to \SI{85}{\percent}; other makers' unmapped cameras remain unmapped.

These are not recent cameras the map has yet to reach: \num{26902} of the \num{44176} carry
the registry's bulk-import date of 26 March 2024, so were on record by then, and only
\num{2150} were recorded in the second half of 2025. A record's creation date is not an
installation date---a device is recorded while planned---and the registry gives no date of
removal, so it cannot be reconstructed as it stood on an earlier day. The trend of
\Cref{sec:results:trend} therefore rests on the map alone.

\subsection{Cone construction}
\label{sec:data:cones}

Each camera head becomes a circular sector: apex at the node, bisector along the tagged
bearing, span \ang{45} (or the tagged arc), radius \SI{60}{\metre}. The radius must reach
across the carriageway from a set-back pole; it was chosen on that reasoning and then swept
over $\{30,45,60,90\}\,\si{\metre}$ (\Cref{sec:lim:geom}). Measured exposure is only mildly
sensitive to it, but the avoidance result is not robust above the assumed value.

All wedges are unioned into a single MultiPolygon of \textbf{\num{135210} parts},
published as one feature with id \texttt{alpr}. Overlapping wedges merging is correct and
intended: the question is \emph{is this road segment observed}, not \emph{by how many
devices}.

A camera with no bearing receives no cone and contributes no exposure: \num{2879} nodes carry
no direction tag and 69 an unparseable one, \num{2948} of \num{142991} (\SI{2.1}{\percent}).
A full circle would overstate a camera that watches one approach; excluding them understates
by at most that share, and makes measured exposure a lower bound on the map's own terms as
well as on the world's. Thirty-one nodes give a full-circle arc (\texttt{0-360}); in this run
the parser gave them a \ang{45} wedge, an error since corrected to a disc of the same radius
and too small---31 of \num{140043} cameras with cones---to move any figure reported here. The
vendor is read from four inconsistently spelled keys by case-insensitive substring
(\num{114360} Flock cameras, against \num{109892} by exact match); it matters only for the
vendor split of \Cref{sec:results:exposure}, and the avoidance model targets the all-vendor
union throughout.

The registry's readers carry no bearing at all, and excluding \num{44176} cameras would
discard the registry. Each receives instead a \textbf{disc of the cone's radius},
\SI{60}{\metre} in every direction, the rule the published device map applies to any device
of unknown heading. A disc watches both carriageways where a Falcon watches one, so it can
overstate a reader's view; \Cref{sec:method:exposure} scores the discs a second way, from the
direction named in the device's label, and \Cref{sec:results:exposure} reports both. The
merged set is the map's wedges, rebuilt with the corrected parser (\num{135202} parts), plus
the discs: \textbf{\num{159286} parts}, one feature with id \texttt{alpr\_merged}, imported
as a graph of its own.

\subsection{Camera maps of earlier dates}
\label{sec:data:history}

OpenStreetMap keeps every edit, so the camera map can be reconstructed as it stood at any
past instant. The public Overpass server answers a query carrying a \texttt{[date:\ldots]}
qualifier from its history: nodes deleted since are restored, nodes added since are absent,
and every tag has its value from that day. Asked for Georgia at the July snapshot's
timestamp it returned \num{9742} nodes, the snapshot's own count to the node. History
queries for the whole continent time out on the public server, so a date is fetched state by
state and merged by node identifier; DeFlock's own Overpass mirror keeps no history and cannot
serve them. They are heavy, and after one complete date the public server began refusing our
connections.

Every other date therefore comes from OpenStreetMap's full-history dump \citep{osmplanet}
(\SI{163}{\giga\byte}, 14 September 2026, checked against its published MD5), read once. Two
passes collect every version of every node that was ever tagged as an \alpr---\num{169565}
nodes and \num{248769} versions, including the later versions that deleted or retagged
them---and the map at any instant is then a filter over that file, with Overpass's semantics:
each node's latest version at or before the instant, kept if it was visible and still tagged.
On the one date both sources cover, 1 July 2025, they agree exactly---\num{22690} nodes, the
same identifiers, the same tags, the same coordinates---and on 1 January 2026 the filter
reproduces an earlier, independent pass over the dump node for node (\num{63526}). The same
file gives the day, to the second, on which each camera entered the map, and a monthly count
of the map that needs no aggregating service.

We reconstruct the map on the first of every month from January 2024 to September 2026,
thirty-three maps, and set them beside the study's snapshots of 24 July and 22 September 2026.
Cones are built from each exactly as from the current map (\Cref{sec:data:cones}). The share
of mapped cameras carrying a bearing, and so receiving a cone, was about \SI{90}{\percent} in
2024 and \SI{98}{\percent} in 2026: the earliest maps are slightly less complete even on their
own terms. Exposure is measured on every monthly map; avoidance is routed on the first of every
quarter (\Cref{sec:method:history}).

One caveat governs everything built on these maps. OpenStreetMap records when a camera was
\emph{mapped}, not when it was installed: an installation date is present on 47 of
\num{142991} nodes. A camera first mapped in 2026 may have been on its pole since 2021. A
past map is therefore what a study run on that day would have seen, and the trend of
\Cref{sec:results:trend} describes how the measured picture changed as volunteers filled
in the map---most of it after DeFlock was founded in October
2024~\citep{deflock}---not how fast cameras were deployed.

\subsection{Road network}
\label{sec:data:roads}

Geofabrik North America extract \citep{geofabrik}, \SI{19.2}{\giga\byte}, retrieved 2026-07-24 and held
fixed for every camera map, so that the trend of \Cref{sec:results:trend} isolates the
camera data: every unavoided route is the same route on every date. Imported with
\texttt{ignored\_highways = footway,\allowbreak construction,\allowbreak cycleway,\allowbreak path,\allowbreak steps}. \texttt{track} is
deliberately retained: unpaved tracks are plausible avoidance routes, and excluding them
would bias the cost estimate downward by removing options a determined driver has.

\subsection{Commute sample}
\label{sec:data:commutes}

Source: LEHD \textbf{LODES8} \citep{lodes8}, file \texttt{od\_main\_JT00}: all jobs with both
ends in-state, built from unemployment-insurance wage records linked to employer
establishments. These are
administrative counts of real jobs, not survey estimates. The year is 2022 for 49 of the 51
jurisdictions; LODES has not published 2022 for Michigan, which uses 2021, or for Alaska,
whose latest year is 2016. LODES8 codes every year against 2020 census geography, so an
older year still names current tracts.

Sampling design carries more weight here than sample size. Uniform random points would
over-represent empty rural space where nobody commutes and no cameras exist, understating
exposure badly. Instead each origin--destination pair is drawn \textbf{with probability
proportional to the number of workers actually making that trip}, so the distribution
approximates what an average \emph{commuter} experiences rather than what an average
\emph{square kilometre} contains.

Block-level LODES geocodes are aggregated to \textbf{tract} (first 11 digits), finer than the
routing's effective precision, and each tract is routed from its 2024 Census Gazetteer internal
point \citep{gazetteer2024} (\texttt{INTPTLAT}/\texttt{INTPTLONG}), which is guaranteed to fall
inside the tract polygon. Same-tract commutes are dropped: origin and destination centroids
coincide, so there is no route to measure. Georgia alone yields \num{1199250} distinct tract pairs
covering \num{4190313} workers; across the 50 states and the District of Columbia the frame
holds \num{132822679} commuters over \num{34420305} tract pairs.

Connecticut needed one repair: it replaced its counties with planning regions in 2022 and every
tract identifier changed, so LODES and the Gazetteer name the same tracts differently and the
state would silently drop out; a crosswalk on the six-digit tract code matches all 879 tracts
in the flows.

\num{1200} pairs are drawn per jurisdiction, \num{61200} in all, with replacement from a
single seeded stream (seed \texttt{20260725}), giving \textbf{\num{58421} distinct commutes}.
The fifteen states of the study's earlier rounds are drawn first, so their \num{17904}
commutes are reproduced exactly and their routes carry over. The sampled pairs represent
\num{2842747} workers; the median pair carries 16 and the largest \num{2832}.

A pair drawn more than once is routed once, since its route is the same, but it stands for
every draw: \textbf{each commute carries its draw count as a weight}. In the fifteen large
states this is immaterial---\SI{0.53}{\percent} of draws are repeats---but in small ones it
is not. Wyoming's \num{1200} draws hold 832 distinct pairs (\SI{30.7}{\percent} repeats),
Alaska's repeat a quarter of the time and the Dakotas' nearly a fifth, and one Vermont pair was
drawn nine times. Collapsing repeats at equal weight, as this study's earlier rounds did,
under-represents precisely the heaviest flows; keeping the counts restores
probability-proportional-to-workers sampling inside every state.

The pairs are what LODES records, not trips anyone has been seen to make. The median routed
commute is \SI{23.6}{\kilo\metre}, but one pair in eight is more than \SI{100}{\kilo\metre}
apart by road (\SI{12.8}{\percent} of commuters, weighted) and one commuter in a hundred more
than \SI{470}{\kilo\metre}: LODES places each job at an employer establishment, imputed where an
employer has several, and pairs that far apart are unlikely to be driven daily. They are kept,
since any cut-off would be arbitrary, and their weight is reported where it matters
(\Cref{sec:results:exposure,sec:lim:scope}).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Two routes per commute}

Only the trip to work is routed. A camera watches one direction of travel, so the journey home
passes a different set of cameras; it is not measured here, and no figure in this paper should
be doubled to stand for a day. Each sampled pair is routed twice against the same graph: a
\textbf{baseline} with no custom model, and an \textbf{avoided} route applying
\texttt{priority: [\{if: "in\_alpr", multiply\_by: 0.01\}]}. Both disable CH, which
per-request custom models require.

Both use GraphHopper's stock car model, which weighs distance as well as time
(\texttt{distance\_\allowbreak influence} 90: a route \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} shorter is preferred if it
costs under \SI{90}{\second} more). The baseline is therefore the router's preferred route
rather than strictly the fastest, and for \SI{3.9}{\percent} of commuters on the merged map
(\SI{4.5}{\percent} on the map alone) the avoiding route---in every such case the longer
one---is the quicker, by a median of \SI{0.9}{\minute}. Extra time is reported as measured,
negative values included; clipping them at zero leaves every median unchanged and raises the
mean extra time from \SI{7.39}{\minute} to \SI{7.45}{\minute} (on the map alone, from
\SI{5.62}{\minute} to \SI{5.70}{\minute}).

\num{0.01} is the application's \emph{strongest} avoidance setting, not a typical one: a
watched road costs a hundred times its usual weight, close to but short of prohibition.
Results therefore describe the \textbf{strong end of both the benefit and the cost} of
avoidance as offered. This is deliberate: it establishes whether meaningful avoidance is
possible at all, and what it costs near the limit.

\subsection{Exposure is directional, not proximate}
\label{sec:method:exposure}

A camera counts as having seen the driver \emph{only if the route line intersects its
cone}. Passing \SI{20}{\metre} from a camera aimed the other way is not exposure.
Implementation is an STRtree over the cone parts for candidate lookup, then an exact
intersection test.

Two simplifications remain, and they pull in opposite directions: route geometry is a
polyline while a vehicle has width, which understates exposure, and a cone occluded by
intervening buildings is still counted as full sight-line, which overstates it.

A registry disc has no direction, so a route that crosses one counts it. The device labels
supply a second, directional, reading: \num{26131} of the \num{44176} name a cardinal
direction of travel (\emph{northbound}, \ldots) and \num{4161} say inbound or outbound. Under
the \emph{directed} scoring a cardinal disc counts only if the route crosses it travelling
within \ang{60} of the named heading, taken from the route's bearing where it passes nearest
the device; the other discs count as before. The two scorings bracket the registry's
contribution: every reader on both carriageways, or every reader on the one its label names.

\subsection{Earlier camera maps need no new baselines}
\label{sec:method:history}

The unavoided route does not depend on the cameras. Routed two months apart against graphs
carrying different camera maps, all \num{17580} commutes of the study's earlier rounds took
identical baseline routes, to the metre and the second. Exposure on an earlier date is
therefore a re-scoring of each saved route against that date's cones, with no routing. The
merged map is treated the same way: the saved baselines re-scored against its cone set, and
avoidance routed against a graph carrying that set for every commute the merged map exposes.

Avoidance does need routing against that date's cameras, and two properties keep it
tractable. First, one graph serves every date: each date's cone set is imported as its own
custom area (\texttt{alpr\_2025\_07\_01}, \ldots), and each request names the one it avoids
---an import of about two hours serves five or six dates rather than one, checked on a test
graph to route each area independently. Second, only commutes that pass a camera on that date are
routed. A route that crosses no cone is already the avoiding route, since the custom model
only makes cone edges dearer and nothing can undercut it; this was checked rather than
assumed, and all \num{3741} such commutes of the fifteen-state run received an identical
avoiding route. The same shortcut serves the main run.

Re-scoring uses route geometry stored at five decimal places, about a metre. Against live
scoring on the current map it moves \SI{1.7}{\percent} of commutes by one camera, in the two
directions about equally, changes whether 5 of \num{17580} (\SI{0.03}{\percent}) pass any
camera at all, and leaves national totals within \SI{0.1}{\percent}. On the full sample the
re-scored national figures match the live ones to the precision reported
(\SI{75.3}{\percent} passing a camera, a mean of \num{5.14}). Within the trend every date,
the current one included, is scored the same way. Exposure is re-scored on every monthly map;
avoidance is routed on the first of every quarter, eleven dates from January 2024 to July 2026,
and on the September 2026 snapshot itself. The 24 July 2026 snapshot enters re-scored only; the
fifteen-state paired comparison covers it (\Cref{sec:results:trend}).

\subsection{Statistical reporting}
\label{sec:method:stats}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medians lead, means accompany.} Both distributions are heavily
    right-skewed; a minority of long commutes through dense corridors pull the mean well
    above the typical experience.
  \item \textbf{National figures are weighted by commuters.} Every jurisdiction was sampled
    at the same size, so an unweighted pool would count Wyoming's \num{198447} in-state
    commuters as heavily as California's \num{16821903}. Each routed commute carries its
    state's commuter total from the LODES frame, shared among that state's commutes in
    proportion to draw count; medians are weighted quantiles, and state figures need only
    the draw counts. Weighting alone moves the share of commuters passing a reader in the
    fifteen states of the earlier rounds from \SI{82.5}{\percent} to \SI{84.3}{\percent} on the
    merged map (from \SI{78.7}{\percent} to \SI{81.1}{\percent} on the map alone),
    because the populous states are the more surveilled ones.
  \item \textbf{Intervals from a bootstrap stratified by state} \citep{efron1993}:
    percentile, \num{2000} replicates, each resampling every state at its own size and
    re-deriving its weights, as the sample was drawn. Commutes from one county share corridors
    and cameras; resampling whole origin counties within states instead \citep{field2007}
    widens the headline intervals two- to three-and-a-half-fold---\SI{78.6}{\percent} becomes
    [77.2--79.4], the median \SI{2.29}{\minute} [2.12--2.56]. Where the two disagree, both
    are shown.
  \item \textbf{Differences between the two maps are paired.} Both runs route the same
    commutes, and the bootstrap draws the same replicates for each, so a difference is
    bootstrapped replicate by replicate rather than read off two separate intervals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Execution}
\label{sec:method:exec}

Twelve concurrent workers against the local routing server, past which contention outweighs
added concurrency. Failed routes are dropped rather than retried, and \textbf{\num{56131} of
\num{58421} commutes routed (\SI{3.9}{\percent} dropped)}. Nine in ten failures are points the
router cannot snap to any road---a tract's internal point deep inside a national park, a
reservation or a wilderness tract---and the rest are genuinely unconnected, such as commutes
between Hawaiian islands. Five states lose more than a tenth of their sample (Alaska
\SI{33}{\percent}, Hawaii \SI{24}{\percent}, Montana and Wyoming \SI{21}{\percent}, New
Mexico \SI{14}{\percent}); the median state loses \SI{2.0}{\percent}. Those five hold
\SI{1.6}{\percent} of the nation's commuters, so the national figures barely register the
loss, but their own estimates describe the routable, probably less remote, part of each
state. A wider road search could snap more of them, but in a test it moved 5 of 100
already-routed commutes to a different road, and the trend of \Cref{sec:results:trend} pairs every saved
route with new ones; consistency was kept over coverage. Commutes are processed in seeded
random order, so any prefix of the output is a near-uniform sample of the whole. Against the
merged map the \num{37725} commutes it exposes were routed again, none failing once 72
interrupted requests were repeated; the two runs' unavoided routes are identical to the metre
and the second, and the merged camera count re-scored from stored geometry matches the
router's on every one.

\subsection{The cost of refusal}
\label{sec:results:cost}

\textbf{\SI{80.8}{\percent} of American commuters could be routed to zero \alpr
exposure}: the fifth whose usual route passes no known reader, and three in four of the rest
(\SI{75.5}{\percent} [74.9--76.1], \Cref{tab:trend}). Over all commuters the avoiding route
adds a median of \SI{2.29}{\minute} and \SI{0.87}{\kilo\metre}, an overhead of
\SI{9.71}{\percent}; for commuters whose usual route passes a camera it adds a median of
\SI{3.92}{\minute} [3.82--4.02]. The median marginal price is \SI{0.83}{\minute} per camera
evaded.

The registry's readers make refusal dearer on every measure. On the same commutes, against the
map alone, the share able to reach zero falls by \num{3.5} points [3.1--3.8], the median extra
time rises by \SI{0.67}{\minute} [0.62--0.72], about two-fifths, and the price of evading a
camera by \SI{0.07}{\minute} [0.06--0.08]. For the commuters the map alone already exposes the
median detour grows from \SI{3.14}{\minute} to \SI{4.12}{\minute}, and \SI{4.5}{\percent} of
all commuters [4.2--4.9] who could reach zero against the map cannot once the registry is
added. Extra time rises for a third of commuters, is unchanged to within three seconds for
three in five, and falls for one in twenty, where the router, finding its old detour watched
too, more often keeps a camera (\SI{43}{\percent} of those routes do, against a quarter of all
exposed commuters' routes).

For most commuters, then, refusing licence-plate surveillance on the way to work costs a few
minutes at most. This is conditional on the assumed camera field of view: it is stable if the
true radius is smaller than the \SI{60}{\metre} assumed here and degrades if it is
substantially larger, which \Cref{sec:lim:geom} quantifies. It is the paper's central
quantitative claim.

The distribution matters as much as the median. The mean overhead is \SI{17.22}{\percent}
against a median of \SI{9.71}{\percent}, and the interquartile range runs from
\SI{0}{\percent} to \SI{24.2}{\percent}: a quarter of commuters pay nothing at all, while a
tail pays substantially. \Cref{fig:route} shows one such case.

State-level variation is extreme (\Cref{fig:states}; every state's figures are tabulated in
the repository). In Georgia \SI{93.9}{\percent} of commuters pass a reader, a mean of
\num{16.6} on the way to work; only \SI{37.5}{\percent} can reach work past none, and avoiding
costs a median of \SI{13.6}{\minute}, an overhead of \SI{44.4}{\percent}. In Illinois
\SI{91.6}{\percent} pass one, in California \SI{89.4}{\percent}, in Florida
\SI{89.1}{\percent}. At the other end \SI{14.8}{\percent} of New Hampshire's commuters pass
one, \SI{12.0}{\percent} of Montana's, \SI{5.4}{\percent} of Vermont's, and none of Alaska's
sampled commuters. In fifteen jurisdictions the median extra time is zero: for at least half
of their commuters the avoiding route takes no longer than the usual one, in eleven of them
because most commuters pass no reader at all. The registry moves some states far more than others, since
it adds only where Flock operates and mappers had not reached: Arizona's share rises from
\SI{72.7}{\percent} to \SI{87.0}{\percent} and Massachusetts's and Wisconsin's by seven points,
while New Hampshire, Vermont, Wyoming and Alaska do not move.

These are differences in the record at least as much as on the roads. Installation density
runs from \num{0.7} per \num{100000} residents in Alaska, and under five in Vermont and New
Hampshire, to \num{158} in Georgia---an eight-fold spread between the \nth{10} and \nth{90}
percentile states---and state exposure tracks it at $r=0.87$. The departures from the line are
what a statewide rate would produce: New York's commuters pass more cameras than its
statewide density predicts, because commuting concentrates where cameras do while the density
is averaged over the whole state; Kansas's pass fewer.

Whether evasion is cheaper per camera where cameras are denser, the data do not settle. On
the map alone, across the 47 states and the District with at least 100 sampled commutes that
evade a camera, the median price per
camera falls weakly as cameras per kilometre rise, from \SI{2.49}{\minute} in New Hampshire to
\SI{0.17}{\minute} in the District of Columbia ($r=-0.29$ against the logarithm of cameras per
kilometre; a Fisher interval excludes zero, $-0.53$ to $-0.01$, but a bootstrap over states
does not, $-0.63$ to $0.23$). On the merged map the correlation is weaker still, $r=-0.19$,
and neither interval excludes zero ($-0.45$ to $0.10$; $-0.58$ to $0.33$). Half the registry's
readers stand in four states the map already showed as heavily watched---Georgia, California,
Texas and Florida---and there the price of evading each rises: Georgia's from
\SI{1.11}{\minute} to \SI{1.32}{\minute}. The extremes hold, New Hampshire dearest and the
District cheapest, but a tendency between them is not one we can claim.

on 24 July and 1 September. Flock's share of the mapped
network went from \SI{17}{\percent} to \SI{71}{\percent} during 2024 and has stayed near
four-fifths since, while the untagged share fell from \SI{81}{\percent} to \SI{5}{\percent}
and the named non-Flock vendors rose from \SI{1}{\percent} to \SI{15}{\percent} as
contributors tagged them.

\paragraph{Exposure followed the map, almost in proportion.} The share of commuters passing
at least one mapped reader crossed a tenth in January 2025, a quarter in June, a half in
December, and stands at three-quarters (\SI{75.3}{\percent}) today; the mean went from
\num{0.05} cameras to \num{5.14}, and adding the registry takes them to \SI{78.6}{\percent} and
\num{6.24}. Against the number of mapped cameras the elasticity is
\num{0.89} across the thirty-five maps. Per camera mapped, exposure has fallen by about a
quarter since January 2024, from \num{0.049} to \num{0.036} encounters per commute per
thousand cameras: the cameras mapped later lie on commute routes slightly less often than
those mapped first, but not much less. Nor is there a sign of saturation. The two months
between the July and September 2026 snapshots alone brought \SI{18.3}{\percent} more mapped
cameras and \SI{17.0}{\percent} more encounters, and the monthly curve of \Cref{fig:trend}b
is still rising by about two points a month.

\paragraph{The states filled in at different times.} In January 2024 Illinois was the only
state where more than a tenth of commuters passed a mapped reader (\SI{23.5}{\percent}); by
January 2025 fifteen were, by July 2025 thirty-one, by January 2026 forty-seven, and today
forty-nine (\Cref{fig:trend_states}). In July 2025 Michigan's commuters were the most exposed
of any state's (\SI{62.8}{\percent}), while Pennsylvania (\SI{2.4}{\percent}), Nevada
(\SI{3.8}{\percent}), New Jersey (\SI{4.4}{\percent}) and North Carolina (\SI{8.2}{\percent})
would have looked nearly camera-free; all four are between \SI{60}{\percent} and
\SI{68}{\percent} today. A state's rank on the January 2025 map predicts its rank today
imperfectly (Spearman $\rho=0.80$ on mean exposure; from January 2024, when 37 jurisdictions
had none, $0.54$; from January 2026, $0.90$). Nor does the map only grow. Wisconsin and Iowa each gained cameras on net
between July and September 2026 yet lost about three points of exposure: 190 and 123 of their
July cameras left the map, deleted or retagged, and the cameras newly mapped added less
exposure than was lost.

\paragraph{Refusal got dearer, and not only because there was more to refuse.} On the
sparse maps of 2024 a commuter who passed a mapped camera could almost always get around it:
on the first, \SI{95.0}{\percent} [92.7--96.9] of exposed commuters could reach zero, for a
median of \SI{0.77}{\minute}, and through 2024 the share held at \SIrange{95}{96}{\percent}
for about a minute. On the September 2026 map \SI{79.0}{\percent} [78.4--79.7] can, for a
median of \SI{3.14}{\minute}, and with the vendor's registry added \SI{75.5}{\percent}
[74.9--76.1], for \SI{3.92}{\minute} [3.82--4.02] (\Cref{tab:trend}; \Cref{fig:trend}c). The
registry moves the present about as far as the preceding months of mapping did: the share
reaching zero by as much as the twelve weeks from the July map, the median detour by as much as
the nearly six months from April's. The quarterly maps show
the shape of the change. The share reaching zero held at \SIrange{95}{96}{\percent} through
2024, stepped down to about \SI{92}{\percent} with the surge of mapping that followed
DeFlock's founding and held there through 2025, and has fallen on every routed map since
October 2025: \SI{91.6}{\percent}, \SI{86.9}{\percent}, \SI{84.7}{\percent},
\SI{82.6}{\percent} and \SI{79.0}{\percent}, each interval clear of the last.
The cost did not step; it climbed. From July 2024 on, the median extra time for an exposed
commuter rose on every routed map, from \SI{1.00}{\minute} to \SI{3.14}{\minute}. Most of
that rise is the number of cameras on each trip, but the price of evading each one rose too,
from \SI{0.42}{\minute} [0.38--0.47] to \SI{0.76}{\minute} [0.75--0.78], and it too rose on
every routed map from July 2024. Two things moved it, and a fixed sample can separate them.
One is who was exposed: each fuller map reached commuters the sparser ones had not, on other
roads with other alternatives. The other happened on the same trips. Followed from the July 2025 map to September
2026's, the \num{12647} commutes exposed in July came to pass \num{9.9} cameras on average
instead of \num{3.2}; the share of them that can reach zero fell from \SI{92.4}{\percent} to
\SI{72.6}{\percent}, and the price per camera rose from \SI{0.65}{\minute} [0.63--0.68] to
\SI{0.81}{\minute} [0.79--0.84]. Every cohort shows it, and for about a year the rise grows
with the time a cohort is followed: the commutes exposed on the July 2026 map pay
\SI{2}{\percent} more per camera twelve weeks later, too little to distinguish; April 2026's
pay \SI{7}{\percent} more, January's \SI{11}{\percent} and October 2025's \SI{20}{\percent},
each interval clear of its starting one. The seven cohorts followed for fourteen months or
more pay \SIrange{21}{35}{\percent} more, six of them clear of their starting intervals and
the seventh, April 2024's, overlapping it slightly. As more of a trip's cameras, and of its
alternatives', are mapped, each costs more to avoid. Over the two months between the 2026
snapshots the change is too small to see, which is why the fifteen-state comparison below
finds the price per camera unchanged.

\paragraph{Fifteen states, fully re-routed.} The trend's last step can be checked against a
comparison that re-routed everything. For the fifteen states of the study's earlier rounds,
the same \num{17580} commutes were routed in full against both 2026 snapshots on the same
road extract, and every baseline route was identical to the metre and the second. Camera
encounters grew by \SI{18.3}{\percent} against \SI{18.7}{\percent} more mapped cameras in
those states, the median price of evading one stayed at \SI{0.78}{\minute} to the second
decimal, and Pennsylvania's mean exposure rose \SI{88.5}{\percent} in two months.

\paragraph{What follows.} Two things. Absolute exposure figures from a volunteer map are a
lower bound that has not fallen from one month's map to the next and will keep rising; one vendor's records alone
raise them again, exposure by a fifth and the price of refusal by two-fifths. Any study built
on crowdsourced surveillance data dates, and should say when its map was drawn. And the price
of refusal dates as well: it has risen on every routed map since July 2024, per camera as well
as in total, and
today's figures describe a record that is still filling in.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Refusal is cheap, and that is the policy-relevant fact}

The strongest result here is also the simplest: \SI{80.8}{\percent} of American commuters
can reach work past no known licence-plate reader, and the avoiding route is a median of
\SI{2.29}{\minute} longer---\SI{3.92}{\minute} for commuters with a camera to avoid. A common
intuition about these networks is that they are, or soon will be, comprehensive enough that
avoidance is futile, so that the privacy loss is unavoidable rather than chosen. At current
density that is false for four commuters in five. It has been false on every map since 2024,
but it is becoming less so: as the map filled in, the share of exposed commuters who can reach
zero fell from \SI{95}{\percent} to \SI{79}{\percent}, and to \SI{75}{\percent} once the
vendor's registry is added, while the median cost for those with a camera to avoid quadrupled
on the map alone and rises by a further quarter with the registry (\Cref{sec:results:trend}).

Cheap refusal also presumes a router that knows where the cameras are. The app built on this
study's cone geometry avoids the volunteer map alone (\Cref{sec:system}). Routes planned around
the map alone are clean of it for \SI{84.2}{\percent} of commuters, but for \SI{35.0}{\percent}
[34.4--35.7] of them the route still passes a reader that only the vendor's registry records,
and for \SI{44.1}{\percent} of those whose usual route passes a mapped camera; \SI{54.7}{\percent}
of commuters get a route clean of both. Avoidance is only as complete as the record it avoids.

The corollary cuts the other way. If avoidance is this cheap, it is also cheap for people
with reason to evade legitimate investigation, which bounds how much investigative value a
fixed-camera network can deliver against a motivated subject while it continues to read
every unmotivated one.

\subsection{Where refusal stops being cheap}

The national figures average over states whose commuters have almost nothing to avoid and one
where avoidance has largely stopped working. In Georgia, the most densely recorded state,
\SI{93.9}{\percent} of commuters pass a reader, the average commute passes \num{16.6}, and only
\SI{37.5}{\percent} can reach work past none; avoiding costs a median of \SI{13.6}{\minute}, an
overhead of \SI{44}{\percent}. On the map alone Georgia's commuters could reach zero in
\SI{54.3}{\percent} of cases, for \SI{7.8}{\minute}: the \num{7031} readers the vendor's
registry adds there turn refusal into something most of them cannot complete. Each camera
there also costs more to avoid than nationally, \SI{1.32}{\minute} against \SI{0.83}{\minute},
which is the cohort finding of \Cref{sec:results:trend} in one place: the more of a trip's
cameras, and of its alternatives', are on the record, the dearer each is to evade.

On the map alone the opposite pattern appears: evasion looks cheapest per camera where cameras
are densest, a pattern that would rest on road-network structure, which OpenStreetMap maps
close to completely, and so might be expected to survive the map's gaps in coverage. The
vendor's registry is a test of exactly that, and the pattern does not survive it: across
states it weakens to a correlation neither interval distinguishes from zero
(\Cref{sec:results:geo}). Which roads are watched is itself a question of coverage, so even a
result built on the road network moves when the record of cameras does.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Tract centroids are not addresses}

Every route runs centroid to centroid; real commutes begin in a driveway. In large or
irregular tracts the centroid can sit far from any residence. This adds noise in both
directions and is the main reason we report distributions rather than point estimates. It
also costs coverage: where a tract's internal point lies too far from any road to snap to,
the commute cannot be routed at all, which removes a fifth or more of the sample in Alaska,
Hawaii, Montana and Wyoming (\Cref{sec:method:exec}).

\subsection{Crowdsourced camera data is non-randomly missing}
\label{sec:coverage}

Absolute exposure figures remain a \textbf{lower bound}, now a tighter one. Like
volunteered geographic data generally \citep{haklay2010,herfort2023}, the map is least
complete where contributors are absent, and there cameras are missing from the map but
present on the pole. Against Flock Safety's own transparency portals the map alone holds, at
the median, \num{0.98} of the cameras that \num{1050} municipal police departments report
operating (\num{28349} mapped inside their limits against \num{31210} reported), small towns
being the least completely mapped \citep{eyesonflock}; against the vendor's registry it holds
\SI{63.8}{\percent} of in-service plate readers (\Cref{sec:data:registry}). The merged map
closes that gap for one vendor, as of December 2025. What it cannot close: other makers'
unmapped cameras, which the registry does not cover; Flock devices installed since December
2025, which the registry predates and the map holds only where mappers have reached them;
and whatever the registry itself omits---Flock has said it operates more than \num{120000}
cameras nationwide \citep{mazurov2026}, against the \num{126389} in-service plate readers
the registry carries, so that gap at least appears small. The trend of
\Cref{sec:results:trend} is the best evidence of how far the map itself still has to go.

\subsection{One vendor's registry, as it stood in December 2025}
\label{sec:lim:registry}

The registry closes the map's largest gap for one vendor at one moment, and inherits both
limits. Its positions are the vendor's records rather than a survey: where a mapped node stands
on the same device the two agree to a median of \SI{5.6}{\metre}, but nothing checks the
\num{44176} readers no mapper has reached. Its statuses are December 2025's, nine months before
the map's snapshot: a reader removed since is still counted, and the \num{34397} the registry
then listed as planned, many presumably installed since, are not. Its readers carry no bearing,
so each is a disc rather than a wedge, which the directed scoring of
\Cref{sec:method:exposure} brackets. The merged figures can therefore err either way: the
discs and any removed readers overstate, while the planned readers installed since and other
makers' unmapped cameras (\Cref{sec:coverage}) understate.

\subsection{Cone geometry: swept, and one result depends on it}
\label{sec:lim:geom}

The \SI{60}{\metre} radius was chosen by reasoning---a pole set back from the kerb needs a
cone long enough to reach the carriageway it watches---and the \ang{45} default span from
the \SI{82}{\percent} of explicit arcs that span exactly that. Neither was measured. We
therefore swept the radius over $\{30, 45, 60, 90\}\,\si{\metre}$, rebuilding the map's cone
geometry at each and re-scoring all \num{56131} routes of the map-alone run
(\Cref{tab:radius}).

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions. Exposure is robust: halving the
radius removes a quarter of it and \SI{90}{\metre} adds \SI{14}{\percent}, so
\Cref{sec:results:exposure} does not rest on the choice. The avoidance headline is conditional
on it, and from above only: routes planned against \SI{60}{\metre} cones stay clean when
re-scored at \SI{45}{\metre} and \SI{30}{\metre}, but at \SI{90}{\metre} the share reaching
zero falls from \SI{83.7}{\percent} to \SI{55.1}{\percent} (\num{-28.7} points,
\SI{95}{\percent} CI $[-29.3, -28.1]$) and residual exposure quadruples. That is a lower
bound on what avoidance could achieve at \SI{90}{\metre}, not an estimate of it: these routes
were planned against \SI{60}{\metre} cones and only re-scored, a router given wider cones
would find different clean routes, and a full re-route at each radius---most of a day
apiece---has not been done. But if real fields of view are half again as long as assumed,
more than a third of the routes counted as clean here are not. The sweep re-scores the map's
wedges; the registry's discs share the radius and have no span to sweep, and the directed
scoring of \Cref{sec:method:exposure} is their sensitivity check.

\subsection{A single avoidance strength}

All results use the strongest setting, \num{0.01}: a watched road costs a hundred times its
usual weight, close to but short of prohibition, so ``zero cameras'' means the optimiser found
a clean path, not that one was guaranteed to exist. Intermediate settings are the practically
interesting ones for a shipped application, and in informal tests whether one took effect
depended on local camera density; a single slider-to-multiplier mapping is unlikely to serve.

\subsection{Sample scope}
\label{sec:lim:scope}

All fifty states and the District of Columbia, but within-state commutes only
(\texttt{od\_main}), so cross-border metropolitan areas---New York/New Jersey,
Philadelphia, Washington, Kansas City, St Louis, Portland---are systematically truncated at
the state line. Several are exactly the dense multi-jurisdiction areas where the question
is most pointed. The District is the extreme case: most people who work there live in
Maryland or Virginia, so its sample describes only commutes with both ends inside the
District. Alaska's flows are from 2016 and Michigan's from 2021, the latest LODES
publishes; the other 49 jurisdictions' are from 2022.

LODES pairs a home with a job's establishment, and one pair in eight in the sample lies more
than \SI{100}{\kilo\metre} apart (\Cref{sec:data:commutes}). Those pairs make the tail, and
they raise the price of refusal more than the share exposed: within \SI{100}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{76.0}{\percent} [75.6--76.5] of commuters pass a reader rather than \SI{78.6}{\percent},
but avoiding costs a median \SI{1.70}{\minute} [1.66--1.77] rather than \SI{2.29}{\minute},
and the mean commute passes \num{4.90} cameras rather than \num{6.24}. The registry's effect
is much the same at that range: on the map alone the figures are \SI{72.5}{\percent} and
\SI{1.20}{\minute}.

\subsection{Past maps record mapping, not deployment}

The trend of \Cref{sec:results:trend} is a trend in what had been mapped. The edit history
gives the day each camera entered the map to the second; it does not give the day the camera
went up, which OpenStreetMap records for 47 of the \num{142257}. A camera first mapped in
2026 may have stood since 2021, and the 128-fold growth of the map cannot be read as a rate of
deployment. Absolute figures date accordingly. The relative claims are the ones to carry
forward, and they held on every map: the cheapness of refusal---under a minute per camera
evaded on all twelve routed maps, though rising on each since July 2024---and the near-proportionality of
measured exposure to the map across all thirty-five. What the maps cannot establish is that the
current one is complete, and the merged map of the present has no past: the registry's record
dates are not installation dates (\Cref{sec:data:registry}).

\subsection{Free-flow travel times}

Routing uses free-flow speeds with no traffic, time-of-day, or turn-restriction penalties,
so both travel times are optimistic. The \emph{difference} between them---the quantity
actually reported---is more robust than either absolute, since both share the bias.

\section{Ethics and dual use}
\label{sec:ethics}

The study uses only public data. The camera locations are volunteers' observations from public
places, already published in OpenStreetMap under an open licence, and the vendor's device
records, already published by an independent researcher \citep{michael2026}, which we use in
aggregate and do not redistribute; the commute flows and
neighbourhood attributes are Census Bureau aggregates, published with the Bureau's
confidentiality protections and attached to census tracts rather than to people; the portal
counts are published by the police departments themselves. No individual is identified, no
one's travel was observed, and the paper adds no camera location to the public record.

The routing engine is dual-use, and we weighed that before publishing it. It lets anyone decline
to be photographed on the way to work, including someone with reason to evade a lawful
investigation. Three considerations decided the matter. The capability is not new: the camera
map is public, and several services already route drivers around it
\citep{deflockmaps,flockhopper,banditkin}. The result it rests on bounds the investigative
value of fixed cameras against a motivated subject whether or not the tool exists
(\Cref{sec:discussion}); what such a network reliably records is the unmotivated majority. And
the measurement itself---how much surveillance an ordinary trip passes, what declining it costs,
and how fast both are growing---is information a public debate about the technology needs and
has not had. The software is released under GPLv3, and its routing server is not exposed to the
internet by default (\Cref{sec:system}).

\section{Reproducibility}

Code, configuration, and the derived outputs behind every table and figure---per-commute
results on every routed date, per-state tables, and every analysis report quoted in the
text---are in the project repository, \url{https://github.com/KaraZajac/TRAMES}, under
\texttt{research/}. Fixed seed \texttt{20260725} throughout, for both sampling and bootstrap.
All external data sources are public and require no API key. Beyond the routing server, the
analysis needs Python with NumPy, Shapely and Matplotlib; pyosmium for the road sample and the
history dump; and pyogrio, which bundles GDAL, only to read the HPMS geodatabase. The
repository's README names the script behind each step, from the camera fetch and the
edit-history extraction through sampling, routing, the past-date runs and the analyses, to
these figures and tables.

\noindent
The \SI{19.2}{\giga\byte} OSM extract, the four \SI{14.7}{\giga\byte} graph caches, the
\SI{163}{\giga\byte} OSM full-history dump, the \SI{1.3}{\giga\byte} of TIGER/Line boundaries and
the \SI{5.6}{\giga\byte} HPMS geodatabase are not redistributed; the acquisition and import scripts rebuild all of them. The
transparency-portal counts behind the coverage check come from a live service and are kept as
retrieved, in \texttt{research/data/validation/}. The registry snapshot is not redistributed:
it is available from its publisher \citep{michael2026}, and the scripts that read it, merge
it with the map and build its discs are in the repository. Commuter weights come from
\texttt{research/out/sampling\_frame.json} and \texttt{sample\_draws.csv}, written by the
sampler.
