## Abstract

*(Written last, once the numbers exist.)*

Two questions, one apparatus:

1. **Exposure.** How many automated license-plate readers can actually see a typical
   American commuter on a typical day?
2. **Cost of refusal.** If that commuter wanted not to be seen, what would it cost them
   in time and distance — and is that cost distributed evenly?

---

## 1. Introduction

Automated license-plate readers (ALPRs) have moved from tollbooths and police cruisers
to residential intersections. A single vendor, Flock Safety, accounts for **82% of the
120,838 ALPR installations mapped in North America** (§3.1). These are fixed, pole-
mounted, and networked; they read every plate that passes, not plates of interest, and
retain the reads.

The public debate about this is conducted almost entirely in the abstract — in terms of
policy, warrants, and retention windows. What is missing is a measurement of the thing
the policy is about: **how much of this a person actually drives through**, and whether
declining to drive through it is a practical option or a theoretical one.

This paper supplies that measurement, and it does so by building the tool the question
implies. We construct a routing engine that models ALPR fields of view as geometry and
can be asked for the fastest route that avoids them. Running every sampled commute
through it twice — once normally, once avoiding — yields both the exposure and the price
of refusing it, on the same road network, for the same trip.

The second question is where the paper earns its keep. A privacy protection that costs
four minutes is a feature. One that costs forty is available only to people with forty
minutes to spare, and a right that is priced is not evenly held. We therefore report
avoidance cost not as a single national figure but across origin-tract income and racial
composition — with the significant caveat developed in §7.2 that the underlying camera
map is crowdsourced, and its blind spots may not be random.

### 1.1 Contributions

- **A directional exposure model.** Cameras are wedges, not circles. 98.7% of mapped
  North American ALPRs carry a bearing tag, so this is feasible at continental scale.
  Proximity-based counting inflates exposure by counting cameras aimed at the opposite
  carriageway; we do not do it.
- **Continental-scale avoidance routing with no forked router.** 114,172 camera cones
  are compiled into a stock GraphHopper spatial index at import time, letting an ordinary
  per-request custom model reference them. No engine patches, no geometry in the request.
- **A commute-weighted rather than area-weighted sample.** 17,904 real home-work pairs
  drawn from LEHD LODES with probability proportional to worker count, across 15 states.
- **An equity analysis of the cost of refusal**, and an honest account (§7.2) of the one
  bias that could overturn it.
- **A vendor-resolved account of exposure.** Flock and the DOT/tolling vendors follow
  different deployment logics — who *buys* surveillance versus where road infrastructure
  sits — and separating them turns "ALPRs correlate with demographics" into a claim about
  a specific procurement model.

## 2. System

### 2.1 Why a fork, and of what

The measurement needs a router that treats "avoid these 114,172 polygons" as a
first-class cost, not a post-hoc filter — and the eventual application needs to be a real
navigation app, not a research script.

**OsmAnd** (GPLv3) is the base. It is the only mature Android navigation client with
first-class OpenStreetMap support, offline maps, and a documented online-routing
extension point. Its *code* is GPLv3; its *artwork* is CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 and is therefore
replaced rather than modified in any derivative.

**GraphHopper 11.0** is the routing engine. **Valhalla was evaluated and rejected**: its
`exclude_polygons` support has open correctness defects (#3266, #4659, #5069) that make
avoidance results unreliable — the failure mode is silently routing through the excluded
region, which is precisely the measurement we depend on.

An architectural note that made the rest of the work tractable: OsmAnd's online routing
engines return **geometry only**. Turn instructions are re-derived client-side from
offline map data via its "attach to the roads" logic. A custom online router therefore
does not have to synthesise navigation instructions — a large amount of work avoided.

### 2.2 The keystone: import-time custom areas

GraphHopper's `custom_areas.directory` resolves GeoJSON into a spatial index **at graph
import time**. We verified empirically — the documentation is ambiguous on this point —
that an area so registered can be referenced by name from a **per-request** custom model:

```json
{ "priority": [ { "if": "in_alpr", "multiply_by": 0.01 } ] }
```

This is what makes the whole design possible without a forked engine. The alternative,
shipping 114,172 polygons in every routing request, is not viable. The cost is that
**regenerating the cones requires a full graph re-import** (~82 min for North America).

Two further configuration decisions are load-bearing:

- **Contraction Hierarchies are disabled** (`profiles_ch: []`). CH bakes edge weights into
  the prepared graph, which is incompatible with per-request avoidance strength. Landmarks
  (LM) are used instead.
- **The `car_alpr` profile shares `car`'s LM preparation** (`preparation_profile: car`).
  This halves preparation time and is *valid* only because avoidance multiplies priority
  strictly **downward**, so the borrowing profile's weights never exceed the preparation
  profile's on any edge — the condition LM correctness requires.

### 2.3 Deployment posture

The routing server binds to localhost. It has **no authentication and no rate limiting**
and is not internet-facing. Any future public deployment must sit behind a reverse proxy
that supplies both.

## 3. Data

All sources are public and key-free. `research/data/fetch.sh` and
`server/alpr/fetch-na-cones.sh` reproduce the acquisition.

### 3.1 ALPR installations

Source: OpenStreetMap, via the Overpass API, selecting
`man_made=surveillance` + `surveillance:type=ALPR`. This is the same corpus surfaced by
the DeFlock project, taken from the upstream rather than a mirror. Retrieved 2026-07-24
in five regional queries (continental US, Alaska, Hawaii, Canada, Mexico), deduplicated
by OSM node id.

**120,838 unique camera nodes.**

| Property | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Any direction tag | 119,249 | 98.7% |
| — via `direction` | 117,356 | 97.1% |
| — via `camera:direction` | 2,285 | 1.9% |
| Multi-head (`;`-separated bearings) | 7,014 | 5.8% |
| Arc-range form (`199-269`) | 4,806 tokens | — |
| `operator` tagged | 22,354 | 18.5% |

The two direction-key rows sum to more than the total: 392 nodes carry both keys.

Manufacturer concentration:

| Manufacturer | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Flock Safety | 99,016 | 81.9% |
| Motorola Solutions | 5,948 | 4.9% |
| Genetec | 2,875 | 2.4% |
| Leonardo | 1,012 | 0.8% |
| Axis Communications | 983 | 0.8% |
| Ubicquia | 568 | 0.5% |

Three tagging realities shaped the parser, each contradicting a plausible assumption:

1. **`direction`, not `camera:direction`.** The OSM wiki documents `camera:direction` for
   surveillance nodes. Mappers overwhelmingly use plain `direction` — by a factor of 51.
   Reading only the documented key would discard 97% of the bearings.
2. **Arc ranges are real and mostly 45°.** 4,806 values give an explicit range rather than
   a bearing. **3,959 of them (82%) span exactly 45°**, which is what justifies 45° as the
   default span for the ~96% of cameras that give a bare bearing. The default is inherited
   from the data, not assumed.
3. **Multi-head units exist** (`320;190`, `0;72;144;216;288`, `199-269;325-35`). 7,014
   nodes. These typically watch *both* carriageways, so silently taking the first bearing
   is the worst possible error — it makes a camera that sees everyone look like one that
   sees half. The parser returns one wedge per head.

### 3.2 Cone construction

Each camera head becomes a circular sector: apex at the node, bisector along the tagged
bearing, span 45° (or the tagged arc), **radius 60 m**. The radius must be large enough to
cross the carriageway from a set-back pole; it was chosen on that reasoning and has **not
been swept empirically** (§7.3).

All wedges are unioned (`shapely.unary_union`) into a **single MultiPolygon of 114,172
parts**, published as one feature with `id: "alpr"`. 119,132 cameras contribute; the
~1,700-node shortfall against §3.1 is nodes whose direction value failed to parse.

Overlapping wedges merging into one part is correct and intended: the question is
*"is this road segment observed?"*, not *"by how many devices?"*

### 3.2b Vendor-split cone sets (RQ5)

The corpus is partitioned into three disjoint cone sets so exposure can be attributed to
a deployment model rather than to ALPR in general:

| Set | Cameras | Cones | Cones/camera | Union parts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flock Safety | 98,882 | 103,564 | 1.05 | 94,474 |
| Other named vendor | 15,135 | 22,643 | **1.50** | 15,902 |
| No vendor tag | 5,115 | 5,574 | 1.09 | 4,565 |

Assigning vendor requires care. Vendor appears under four keys
(`manufacturer`, `surveillance:manufacturer`, `brand`, `surveillance:brand`) and is spelled
inconsistently. An exact match on `manufacturer == "Flock Safety"` finds 95,069 cameras;
a case-insensitive substring search across all four finds **99,650**. The 4,581 difference
is mostly 4,812 nodes carrying the vendor under `brand`, plus variants ("Flock Group Inc.",
"Flock Safety Inc", "FlockSafety", "flock"). Misfiling 4.6% of Flock cameras as non-Flock
is the one error a vendor comparison cannot absorb, so the substring matcher is used.

Untagged cameras are held out of **both** vendor sets rather than assigned to the
complement of Flock, since an untagged camera is more likely to be an unlabelled Flock
unit than a non-Flock one. They are reported as their own set so nothing is silently
dropped, with the caveat that their direction coverage is lower (88.4% vs 99.3% for Flock
and 98.5% for other named vendors) and their cones are correspondingly fewer.

The cones/camera column is itself a result: **non-Flock installations are multi-head
nearly eight times as often** (24.0% vs 3.1%), consistent with DOT and tolling gantries
covering every lane while a Flock unit watches one approach.

### 3.3 Road network

Geofabrik North America extract, 18 GB, retrieved 2026-07-24. Imported with
`ignored_highways: footway,construction,cycleway,path,steps`. `track` is deliberately
retained — unpaved tracks are plausible avoidance routes and excluding them would bias the
cost estimate downward by removing options a determined driver has.

### 3.4 Commute sample

Source: LEHD **LODES8**, `od_main_JT00`, 2022 — all jobs, within-state.

Sampling design carries more weight here than sample size. Uniform random points would
over-represent empty rural space where nobody commutes and no cameras exist, understating
exposure badly. Instead each origin-destination pair is drawn **with probability
proportional to the number of workers actually making that trip**, so the distribution
approximates what an average *commuter* experiences rather than what an average *square
kilometre* contains.

Block-level LODES geocodes are aggregated to **tract** (first 11 digits) — the finest
resolution at which ACS demographics are reliable, and finer than the routing's effective
precision anyway. Same-tract commutes are dropped (origin and destination centroids
coincide; there is no route to measure).

1,200 pairs are requested per state; duplicate draws are collapsed, costing 0.53%, for
**17,904 total** across 15 states (AZ, CA, CO, FL, GA, IL, MO, NC, NY, OH, PA, TN, TX, VA,
WA). Seed `20260725`; the sample is reproducible. The sampled pairs represent 600,899
workers; the median pair carries 10 and the largest 2,832.

Because draws are taken with replacement and then deduplicated, each pair appears once and
every row is weighted equally in the analysis. That is valid only while inclusion
probability remains proportional to worker count, which decays once a pair is likely to be
drawn more than once. Measured on Georgia — the largest state sample — the single
highest-flow pair has an expected 0.22 draws per 1,200 and a median pair 0.0036, so the
design is probability-proportional-to-size with distortion confined to the extreme upper
tail (roughly 11% under-representation for the single largest pair, negligible below it).
The `workers` and `flow_share` columns are carried through to the output but deliberately
unused downstream: equal weighting already yields the worker-weighted distribution, and
weighting again would apply the flow twice.

### 3.5 Demographics and geography

- **Tract centroids:** 2024 Census Gazetteer internal points (`INTPTLAT`/`INTPTLONG`).
- **Income:** ACS 5-year 2022, table **B19013** (median household income).
- **Race/ethnicity:** ACS 5-year 2022, table **B03002** — chosen over B02001 because it is
  Hispanic-aware, so "non-Hispanic Black" and "non-Hispanic White" are directly available
  rather than reconstructed.

Demographics are attached to the **origin (home) tract**. The claim under test concerns
who lives where surveillance is dense, so the residential end is the right anchor.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Two routes per commute

Each sampled pair is routed twice against the same graph:

- **Baseline** — profile `car`, no custom model.
- **Avoided** — profile `car`, custom model
  `priority: [{if: "in_alpr", multiply_by: 0.01}]`.

Both use `ch.disable: true` (LM/flexible mode), required for per-request custom models.

**0.01 is the maximum avoidance strength**, not a typical setting. Results therefore
describe an **upper bound on both the benefit and the cost** of avoidance — the strongest
available preference short of a hard prohibition. This is deliberate: it establishes
whether meaningful avoidance is possible at all, and what it costs at the limit.
Intermediate strengths are the subject of §7.4.

### 4.2 Exposure is directional, not proximate

A camera counts as having seen the driver **only if the route line intersects its cone**.
Passing 20 m from a camera aimed the other way is not exposure.

Implementation: an STRtree over the 114,172 cone parts for candidate lookup, then an exact
`intersects` test. This is the conservative choice — proximity counting would inflate every
exposure figure in the paper and overstate its central claim.

Two known conservatisms remain, both understating exposure:

- Route geometry is a polyline; a vehicle has width.
- A cone clipped by intervening buildings is still counted as full sight-line.

### 4.2b Vendor attribution costs no additional routing

RQ5 asks whether Flock's cameras reach different people than other vendors'. Routing does
not change — the avoidance model targets the all-vendor union either way — so the question
is entirely a matter of re-scoring existing routes against different cone sets.

This is only possible because the experiment persists route polylines to a gzipped
sidecar. Keeping only the counts would have made RQ5, and the cone-radius sweep of §7.3,
each a fresh multi-hour re-run. The sidecar is written and flushed per record; because a
flush does not emit a gzip end-of-stream marker, it is read through a tolerant
decompressor that ignores a trailing partial line, so it survives an interrupted run and
can be analysed while one is in progress.

Consistency check: the three vendor sets should sum to slightly **more** than the
all-vendor union, because cones from different vendors that overlap merge into a single
part in the union and are counted once, while the split sets count them separately. On the
partial sample the split sum exceeds the union by 1.0% — the right sign and a plausible
magnitude. An earlier check that omitted the untagged set produced a *deficit*, which was
the untagged cameras going uncounted rather than a scoring fault.

### 4.3 Statistical reporting

- **Medians lead, means accompany.** Both distributions are heavily right-skewed; a
  minority of long commutes through dense corridors pull the mean well above the typical
  experience. Reporting only the mean overstates the typical burden; only the median hides
  the tail.
- **Bootstrap confidence intervals** (percentile, 2,000 resamples, seed `20260725`).
  Camera counts are discrete and zero-inflated, nowhere near normal.
- **Quartile contrasts, not regressions.** With tract-level aggregates, a regression
  coefficient invites a causal reading the design cannot support. Quartile contrasts state
  only what is observed.
- **Ecological inference is not attempted.** Tract composition is a property of tracts, not
  of the individuals routed. See §7.1.
- **Total exposure and per-kilometre density are reported as peers.** Longer commutes pass
  more cameras trivially, so a raw count gap that vanishes once normalised per km is about
  distance, not siting. But the converse case is a finding rather than a nuisance: a group
  whose commutes are *shorter but more densely watched* shows a rate gap and no count gap,
  and treating the rate purely as a control on the count would discard it. Both are
  reported with bootstrap CIs for all four combinations of significance. Cameras/km is
  computed as a ratio of sums (total cameras ÷ total km) and bootstrapped over commutes;
  a mean of per-commute rates would let very short commutes dominate.

### 4.4 Execution

12 concurrent workers against the local routing server. Worker count was measured, not
assumed: 12 workers completed 300 commutes in 324 s (56/min), while 32 workers took longer
than 576 s on the identical set — past ~12, GIL contention in the geometry test and load on
the routing server outweigh the added concurrency.

Failed routes are **dropped, not retried**. Failures are dominated by unroutable centroid
pairs — islands, centroids landing off-network — which are properties of the sample rather
than transient faults, so retrying would spend time to fail identically. The drop rate is
reported with the results.

Records are appended and flushed individually. An earlier run of this experiment held all
results in memory and wrote only after the final row; it was killed at 10,500/17,904 and
lost every one. The current runner also supports `--resume`, keyed on
`(state, h_tract, w_tract)`.

Commutes are processed in **seeded random order** rather than file order. The sample is
grouped by state and the executor preserves input order, so in file order the results
arrive one state at a time: an interruption would leave some states complete and others
entirely absent, and no partial output could support a national estimate. Shuffling makes
any prefix of the output a near-uniform sample of the whole. This has no effect on the
completed result set — only on what is salvageable if the run does not complete, and on
the ability to exercise the analysis against representative data while it is still going.

### Prior expectation, recorded before the data

Continental spot-checks during system validation gave 100% avoidance on every route
tested, at these costs:

| Route | Cameras | Extra time | min/camera |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlanta metro | 8 → 0 | +2.6 min | 0.33 |
| Chicago metro | 6 → 0 | +1.9 min | 0.32 |
| Phoenix metro | 1 → 0 | +0.4 min | 0.38 |
| Wilmington → Dover | 6 → 0 | +5.4 min | 0.89 |
| Atlanta → Athens | 10 → 0 | +13.5 min | 1.35 |

On RQ5, a first scoring of 369 routes shows Flock supplying **65.6% of exposures while
holding 82.5% of installations** — non-Flock cameras generating roughly **2.6× more
commute exposure per unit deployed**. The mechanism is siting: DOT and tolling cameras sit
on the highways commuters use, and are multi-head, while Flock units watch residential
approaches that fewer commuters cross. No demographic contrast is distinguishable at this
sample size; all of them are reported here only to show the apparatus runs, not as
findings.

These 5 routes are not a sample and are reported only as the expectation the full run will
confirm or refute. They already overturned one intuition: we expected dense metros to be
the *expensive* place to avoid cameras, and the opposite holds. Grid networks offer
parallel alternatives a block away; rural and intercity routes have one road, so avoidance
means a genuine detour. **If this survives the full sample, the headline is that avoidance
is cheap where cameras are dense and expensive where they are sparse** — the reverse of the
natural guess, and consequential, because it means the burden of refusal falls hardest on
rural drivers who have the fewest cameras to refuse.

## 7. Limitations

Stated plainly, because two of these are serious enough to bound what the paper may claim.

### 7.1 Tract centroids are not addresses

Every route runs centroid-to-centroid. Real commutes begin in a driveway. In large or
irregular tracts the centroid can sit far from any residence, and occasionally off the road
network entirely. This adds noise in both directions and is the main reason the paper
reports distributions rather than point estimates for individual commutes.

The related and stricter caution: tract demographics describe **tracts, not the people
routed**. "Commutes originating in high-poverty tracts pass more cameras" is supportable.
"Poor people pass more cameras" is not — it is the ecological fallacy, and the design
cannot license it.

### 7.2 Crowdsourced camera data may be non-randomly missing — the load-bearing caveat

The ALPR corpus is contributed by volunteers. **Where contributors are absent, cameras are
absent from the map but present on the pole.** Absolute exposure figures are therefore a
**lower bound**.

The real hazard is not undercounting; it is that undercounting may **correlate with the
very demographics under study**. If mapping effort concentrates in affluent, tech-engaged,
politically-activated communities, then a measured "high-income tracts have more cameras"
finding could be an artefact of who is doing the mapping, and the true gradient could be
flat or inverted. This threat applies to §5.4 and §5.5 specifically and to no other result
in the paper.

We do not have an independent ground-truth census to correct against. Three partial
defences, all of which will be reported:

1. **Within-county replication (§5.6).** Quartiles are recomputed *within each county* and
   pooled, so tracts are ranked only against neighbours subject to the same local mapping
   effort. Counties with fewer than 40 sampled commutes are excluded, and the share of the
   sample retained is reported — a check that silently covered a third of the data would
   be worse than none. County is a compromise: it is available directly from the tract
   GEOID with no crosswalk, but it is smaller than the labour market a commute actually
   spans, and large metros are split across many counties.

   **This check is not clean, and its result must not be read as though it were.**
   Ranking within county holds mapping intensity roughly constant, but it also holds
   *urbanicity* roughly constant, and the two are confounded. Nationally, high-%Black
   tracts are disproportionately urban and urban roads carry more cameras per km, so a
   national gradient that vanishes within county is consistent with at least three
   distinct stories: differential mapping effort, an urban/rural composition effect, or
   genuine between-jurisdiction differences in procurement. This design cannot separate
   them. What the check does establish is whether a gradient is a *between-county* or
   *within-county* phenomenon — which is worth knowing, and is much less than a
   attribution of cause.

   A further limit: only counties with at least 40 sampled commutes qualify, and those are
   the denser ones. The within-county pool is therefore a more urban subsample than the
   national one — visible in its per-km rates, which sit above the national Q1 rate for
   every quartile. The share of the sample it covers is reported alongside every result.
2. **Per-km normalisation (§4.3)** separates "longer commute" from "denser siting."
3. **Directional statement of the residual risk** rather than a claim of correction.

An early indication, on partial single-state data and therefore not yet a result: the
national gradients (income 0.84×, %Black 1.24×) both flatten toward unity under
within-county ranking (1.03×, 0.91×). If that holds on the full sample, it is evidence
that the national demographic contrasts are substantially an artefact of *where people
map cameras* rather than where cameras are — and the paper will say so.

Any demographic finding in this paper is stated as conditional on the coverage assumption,
and that condition is not a formality.

### 7.3 Unswept geometric parameters

The 60 m cone radius and the 45° default span were chosen by reasoning (pole set-back;
82% of explicit arcs) rather than sensitivity analysis. The exposure counts scale with
both. A sweep over radius ∈ {30, 45, 60, 90} m would establish how much the headline
numbers depend on the choice; it has not been run.

### 7.4 A single avoidance strength, and a slider that does not generalise

All results use `multiply_by = 0.01`, the maximum. Intermediate strengths are the
practically interesting ones for a shipped application, and they behave badly: the usable
range is roughly 0.3 → 0.01, and **the dead zone is density-dependent**. A setting of 0.3
does nothing in Delaware while avoiding 62.5% of cameras in Atlanta. A single fixed
slider-to-multiplier mapping in the user interface is therefore not viable; the mapping
must adapt to local camera density. This is an open problem for the application and an
unmeasured dimension of this paper.

### 7.5 Sample scope

15 states, not 50. Within-state commutes only (`od_main`), so cross-border metros —
NYC/NJ, Philadelphia, Washington DC, Kansas City, St. Louis, Portland — are systematically
truncated at the state line. Several of these are exactly the dense multi-jurisdiction
areas where the question is most pointed.

### 7.6 Static snapshot; no temporal dimension

One 2026-07-24 snapshot of a network under rapid expansion; results date quickly. Routing
uses free-flow speeds with no traffic, time-of-day, or turn-restriction penalties, so both
baseline and avoided travel times are optimistic. The *difference* between them — the
quantity the paper actually reports — is more robust than either absolute, since both
share the bias.

### 7.7 Avoidance is modelled as a routing preference, not a guarantee

`multiply_by: 0.01` makes observed roads expensive, not impassable. Where no alternative
exists, the router uses the watched road, correctly. "Zero cameras" results mean the
optimiser found a clean path, not that one was guaranteed to exist.
